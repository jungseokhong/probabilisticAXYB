Error scales

Three different things, all called “error”

These get conflated easily and they are orders of magnitude apart. Reading one for another is the most common way to misjudge this dataset.

Non-repeatability is real, and it is not sensor noise. Each window averages ~1200 mocap frames, so what actually enters B is the third-decimal column — about 0.001 mm. The repeat-to-repeat scatter is ~100× larger. The robot does not return to the same pose. Forward kinematics cannot see this at all: its own repeat scatter is 0.007–0.046 mm, purely encoder repeatability, because the encoders return to the same counts whether or not the link does.

Session drift does not explain it either — differences between epochs account for only 5–6% of the variance. Nor does the approach path: the visit order was shuffled, giving ~4.7 distinct predecessors per configuration, and a regression from the predecessor's pose to the error scores -0.03 to 0.00. Whatever causes it is not the previous pose.

What the residual is made of

The latent pose C, and why the noise model decides the answer

Configuration 2 posits a latent true pose Cᵢ per measurement and writes the error as an FK part composed with a mocap part: Nᵢ = X Cᵢ⁻¹ Aᵢ, Mᵢ = Cᵢ⁻¹ Y Bᵢ, and algebraically the loop-closure residual is exactly the two composed. Cᵢ is not bookkeeping: it is base_T_rigid, where the marker cluster actually was.

C sits between the robot's view (A X) and mocap's view (Y B), and it hugs mocap — 0.14 mm away from it against 3.05 mm from the robot at the end effector. That is the noise model doing its actual job: deciding where the truth lies between two instruments that disagree. Change the anchor and C slides toward whichever you declared more reliable, which is exactly why anchoring one side is required rather than optional.

The split also settles what the residual is: |N| dwarfs |M| everywhere except link_right_arm_2, whose FK estimate is pinned at the mocap floor. The residual is forward-kinematics error almost entirely.

Neither deferral is a data‑collection mistake — both are structural. link_right_arm_0 is the first link of the arm, so its pose changes only when joint 0 turns, and joint 0 turns about one fixed axis. No amount of arm motion can give it a second axis; its origin does not translate either, because the joint axis runs through it. The torso is the more obvious case — it never moved at all.

The fix is upstream of the solver. Moving the torso during collection would compose torso rotations with joint 0’s, giving link_right_arm_0 genuine multi‑axis excitation and letting it determine Y on its own. Worth doing on the next capture, since it would also let the torso itself be calibrated rather than deferred.

Before excitation was checked, both targets returned healthy‑looking residuals — 0.42° / 0.96 mm for link_right_arm_0 — alongside a Y that sat 24 m and 42 m away. A low residual proves a fit, not an identifiable one.

Data-quality caveat on link_right_arm_5. A note in the bag directory records that mocap kept losing track of links 4 and 5, and the numbers agree: it carries the thinnest marker coverage of any target (median 3.84 valid markers against 4.9–5.7 elsewhere) and 17 of its 100 windows were dropped. Its influence on the answer is small — excluding it moves Y by 0.046° / 1.23 mm, inside the uncertainty — so it is retained, but it is the first target to drop if a result looks suspect. link_right_arm_4 is absent from the dataset entirely.

Reading the table

What each column measures

Excitation
How many independent directions the target’s rotations cover. Take every relative rotation A₀⁻¹Aᵢ, reduce each to its unit rotation axis, stack the axes and take the SVD; excitation is the ratio of the second singular value to the first. All axes parallel gives 0; axes spread through space give 0.3–0.7. It is dimensionless. Why it matters: if every rotation is about one axis, you can turn X about that axis and counter‑turn Y by the same amount and the data fits exactly as well — an entire one‑parameter family of solutions, none distinguishable from another.
X translation
Where the mocap rigid body’s origin sits relative to the link’s own origin, in the link’s coordinates. A physical offset — link_torso_5 at (72.25, −2.09, 89.78) mm means the marker cluster sits about 72 mm along the link’s x and 90 mm along its z. You could sanity‑check it against the robot with a ruler.
X rotation rpy
The same transform’s orientation, as roll–pitch–yaw about x, y, z. How the marker cluster’s axes are turned relative to the link’s axes. The specific values look arbitrary because they are: mocap software assigns a rigid body’s axes however it likes when the body is defined. Use the rotation matrix in code, not these angles — rpy is a display convenience and degenerates near ±90° pitch.
FK σ pos
The forward‑kinematics position error the solver converged on for that link, and the value it fed back into the weighting. Estimated from the residual, pooled across all of that target’s poses. floor marks a target where it hit the 0.3 mm mocap anchor — the residual could not resolve anything smaller.
Measured err
The honest one. For every pair of poses, Aᵢ⁻¹Aⲧ and Bᵢ⁻¹Bⲧ describe the same physical motion in two frames, so their displacements along the screw axis must be equal. This is the median disagreement. Neither X nor Y appears in it, so no calibration choice can improve it — it is how inconsistent FK and mocap simply are.
Residual
After solving, how far AᵢX lands from YBᵢ, in translation, RMS over every pose. Larger than the measured error because one rigid (X, Y) pair has to fit all poses at once. For the two stage‑2 rows this column instead shows scatter — the spread of per‑pose X estimates about their mean, which is a consistency check, not a fit error, and the two are not comparable.
σ vs residual
These measure the same error in different units, which is why the columns look further apart than they are. FK σ is a per‑axis standard deviation — a model parameter, pooled from the Nᵢ translations and fed back as the weighting. Residual is the length of a 3‑D vector — a direct measurement, no model involved. For isotropic error the two differ by √3: at the end effector 1.834 mm per axis becomes 3.177 mm of vector length, against a measured residual of 3.135 mm. Judge the fit by the residual, which is assumption‑free; read σ as what the solver believes about the FK side after attributing the rest to mocap.
The iteration
FK noise is estimated from the fit and fed back into it: solve → split the error into N and M → re-estimate ΣN from N → solve again. Note it is N that returns, not the raw residual — feeding back the residual would count mocap error as FK error. ΣM is never re-estimated, and that fixed anchor is what gives the loop a unique fixed point instead of letting both covariances drift together. The first pass matters most: Y moves 9.3 mm as the weighting corrects itself, then it converges geometrically and is below 0.25 mm by the third pass. Three iterations is enough; past five you are refining digits the ~1.7 mm calibration uncertainty makes meaningless.

Forward-kinematics noise

The error grows with depth in the kinematic chain

Two independent measurements of the same thing. Measured error compares Aᵢ⁻¹Aⲧ against Bᵢ⁻¹Bⲧ — these are conjugate, so their screw‑axis displacements must agree, and the disagreement involves neither X nor Y. Fitted FK σ is what the solver settled on after five reweighting passes. They agree, and both rise monotonically down the arm — the signature of accumulating kinematic‑model error rather than sensor noise.

Rotational FK σ pinned at exactly 0.500° on four of five targets — the value of the mocap anchor. Forward‑kinematics rotation error is real, but it sits below what this motion capture system can resolve, so the residual cannot measure it. That is a statement about what is observable, not a solver failure.

How the noise model was set

Anchor one side, estimate the other

Only the ratio of the two covariances is identifiable, so one side has to be fixed at a trusted value or the problem has no unique answer. Motion capture is the side with a specification, so it is the anchor; forward kinematics is whatever is left in the residual.

0.3 mm / 0.5°

Mocap — fixed

Taken from the manufacturer specification and never re‑estimated. It doubles as the floor on the FK estimate: the residual cannot resolve an error smaller than the instrument that observed it.

0.3 – 1.8 mm

Forward kinematics — estimated

Re‑estimated each pass as the covariance of the residual, pooled across all ~100 poses of a target. Converges by the third pass.

16×

Why not repeat variance

Repeat spread measures precision, not accuracy. The torso reads 0.004 mm of FK motion while mocap sees 0.379 mm — 16× its own noise floor, and repeatable per arm configuration. The torso flexes under the arm.

The per‑pair precision matrices in the NPZ files are deliberately ignored. Estimated from 2–5 repeats each, they are rank deficient by construction — two samples give a rank‑one 3×3 — and inverting them, even with an eigenvalue floor, produces near‑hard constraints along essentially arbitrary directions. They fit measurably worse than a plain isotropic weighting.

Reading the uncertainty

Use the cross-target spread, not the reported sigma

1.66 mm

Cross-target spread, in the workspace

Five targets independently estimate the same physical Y. How much they disagree, where the robot actually works. This is the number to quote.

1.34 mm

Reported σ — optimistic

What the covariance formula claims, at the mocap origin where the spread is 5.75 mm. It assumes zero‑mean independent noise; systematic FK bias is neither, so it understates by about 4×.

The cross‑target check works precisely because mocap is common to every target. A mocap error shifts all five estimates identically and produces zero spread; only the FK chain differs per target. So this spread is nearly blind to mocap error and sensitive to exactly the error that dominates here.

What it cannot do is remove that error. Reweighting downweights A correctly and yields honest uncertainty, but the bias stays in X and Y. Recovering those millimetres needs kinematic parameter identification — and this dataset, with six links plus the end effector observed by mocap across twenty configurations, is close to what that requires.
